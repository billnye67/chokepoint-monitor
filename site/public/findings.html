Ships come back for deals, not for quiet

James Boger, 27 November 2026. Ship counts are IMF PortWatch daily transits, data to 22 November 2026.

Shipping does not return just because the fighting slows. At the Bosporus, attack reports have fallen for four months and the ships have not come back. At Hormuz, they came back within days of an agreement and left when it broke down.

My read

1. The Bosporus will not recover on its own. (Medium confidence.) Attack reports at Black Sea ports peaked in July and have fallen every month since, but ship traffic kept falling, from about 82 ships a day in June to 48 in November, a fall of about 40%. I am wrong if traffic climbs back above 70 ships a day with no ceasefire or shipping deal.

2. A deal at Hormuz would show up in the ship counts within days. (Medium confidence.) The 7-day average rose from 4 to 32 ships a day in the twelve days after the 17 June agreement, then fell back when conflict reports rose. The agreement date is from Al Jazeera. I am wrong if an agreement is announced and the 7-day count stays flat for two weeks.

3. Most of the oil is not going around. (Medium confidence.) The Cape of Good Hope gained about 2 tankers a day while Hormuz lost about 35, and Malacca tankers are down about 12%. Cape tankers did rise in spring, and the monitor flagged them as above normal from 25 March to 4 May. They have been level with last year since July. I am wrong if Cape tankers climb back above their spring level of 23 a day and stay there while Hormuz is shut.

The evidence

1. The Bosporus lost about 40% of its traffic and no alarm went off

Bosporus traffic fell from about 82 ships a day in June to 48 in November, a fall of about 40%. A year ago it was 94.7; it is now 49.0.

The monitor rated this "elevated" from 24 July to 9 November and never "abnormal". The fall was gradual, so the normal range followed it down. That is a known weakness of the rules and is listed on the method page.

What else changed over the same months: reported attacks on Black Sea ports rose sharply, then eased. GDELT, a public database of news events, logs 342 conflict events at Black Sea ports and waters in June 2026, 1,227 in July, 1,146 in August, 876 in September, 711 in October and 424 in November to the 22nd. Most are located at Odesa and Novorossiysk. These counts keep only places in Ukraine, Russia, Turkey, Romania, Bulgaria, Georgia and the sea itself.

2. Malacca tanker traffic is down about 12%

Tankers through the Malacca Strait averaged 72.6 a day from March to November 2026, against 82.8 over the same months of 2025. Total Malacca traffic stayed inside its normal range, so this never raised a flag. The timing matches the Hormuz closure. The data cannot show the cause.

3. Total traffic did not reroute, but Cape tankers rose in spring

When ships avoided the Red Sea in 2024, traffic moved to the Cape of Good Hope, and the monitor's log shows that shift. Total traffic did not move that way this time. From March to November the Cape averaged 89.9 ships a day against 90.7 a year earlier. Suez averaged 41.5 against 38.9, which is up 6.7%, not flat. The monitor logged no rerouting signal.

Tankers are the exception. Cape tankers averaged 19.5 a day from March to November against 17.2 a year earlier. The rise came in spring: April averaged 23.0 against 17.1, and the monitor flagged Cape tankers as above normal from 25 March to 4 May. Since July they have been level with last year.

The scale is small next to the loss. The Cape gained about 2 tankers a day. Hormuz lost about 35. A ship that cannot leave the Gulf has no other strait to use. The alternatives are pipelines to ports outside the strait, such as Fujairah and Yanbu, and this data does not cover those ports yet.

4. The June rebound was real, partial and short

The Hormuz 7-day average rose from 4.0 on 17 June to a peak of 31.9 on 29 June, then fell back to about 23 by early July and 5.6 by 20 July. Single days reached 51 and 46 ships on 24 and 25 June.

Al Jazeera reports that an interim agreement on 17 June lifted daily traffic to about 20 ships. GDELT shows cooperative news events at Hormuz peaking on 17 and 18 June, then conflict events rising from 25 to 28 June, including a reported attack on a cargo ship and US strikes in response. MarineLink reports that by 17 July renewed attacks and a resumed US blockade had cut transits to three in a day.

Even at its peak the rebound stayed well below the bottom of the normal range, which is 52.5.

5. The busiest week of the year came right before the war

The final week of February 2026 was the busiest of the year at Hormuz. Traffic averaged 92.7 ships a day from 22 to 28 February and reached 116 on 25 February, against 67.8 for January and February as a whole. The war began on the 28th.

The data cannot show why that week was so busy.

Background: the collapse itself

The war began on 28 February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran (Gulf News). The drop shows in the data from 1 March: 57 ships on 28 February, 20 on 1 March, 4 on 2 March. The monitor's anomaly opens on 3 March.

  • Hormuz averaged 67.8 ships a day in January and February 2026, 5.9 a day from 3 March to 22 November, and 3.2 over the last 30 days. The same March to November window in 2025 averaged 93.1.
  • The 7-day average has been below the normal range on all 265 days since 3 March, and at the abnormal level on 256 of them. The other 9 are the first days of March, while the average was still falling, and the days around the late-June rebound.
  • Tankers fell from 37.0 a day in January and February to 2.2 since 3 March, and 0.8 over the last 30 days. 14 of the 265 days show no transits at all.
  • Traffic was not clearly weak before the war. January 2026 averaged 58.5 against 70.5 in January 2025, but February 2026 averaged 78.3 against 73.9.

What could make this wrong

  • Two cases is a small sample. One strait and one sea do not make a rule.
  • Timing is not proof of cause. The data shows what changed and when. It does not show why.
  • The Hormuz rebound also overlapped a quiet stretch in the news, so that case alone can't separate a deal from a lull. The Bosporus is the case that carries the argument.
  • Ship counts are not barrels. A count of tankers says nothing about how full they were or how much oil moved by pipeline.
  • The ship counts are estimates. PortWatch builds them from ship-tracking signals and misses ships that switch their transmitters off.
  • Levels differ by counting method. PortWatch shows about 68 to 93 ships a day at Hormuz before the war; news reports cite 100 to 140. This page uses PortWatch throughout so that comparisons are like for like.
  • GDELT counts news reports of events, not verified events. Heavier coverage raises the count.
  • The Hormuz drop was checked against independent reporting. Lloyd's List figures reported by Gulf Business give 181 vessels for 1 to 30 March 2026. Vortexa reports tanker transits falling from nearly 40 a day to a few. Al Jazeera reports a fall from about 100 ships a day to an average of five. Direction and scale agree.
